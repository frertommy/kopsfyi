01What PKMN-VNTG is

PKMN-VNTG is the broad benchmark for original-era Pokémon — the Wizards of the Coast print run that built the hobby: Base Set, Jungle, Fossil, Team Rocket, the Gym and Neo series, Legendary Collection. It is the asset class people mean when they say "vintage Pokémon," packaged as one diversified, tradeable number rather than a single Charizard bet.

Vintage is the hobby's store of value — scarce, finished supply, nostalgia-anchored demand. It is also the hobby's high-beta leg: thinner, higher-vol, and driven by completely different forces than Modern.

Like its sister index, PKMN-VNTG optimizes for three properties: correlation with the broad vintage market, controlled movement, and no single card or character dominating the level. But the way it has to be built is the opposite of Modern — and understanding why is the whole point of this page.

02The universe — what counts as "Vintage"

Era is assigned structurally from each card's set. Vintage is the WOTC era — English sets from 1999 through 2003, before The Pokémon Company took printing in-house:

  • Base Set, Base Set 2, Jungle, Fossil (1999–2000)
  • Team Rocket, Gym Heroes, Gym Challenge (2000)
  • Neo Genesis → Neo Destiny, Legendary Collection (2000–2002)
  • the e-Card series — Expedition, Aquapolis, Skyridge (2002–2003)

This is a finished supply — nothing new is ever printed, populations only grow through fresh grading submissions, and the iconic cards are culturally cemented. That fixed-supply, demand-driven character is what makes vintage the store-of-value sleeve.

03The structural twist — why Vintage can't be built like Modern

The obvious way to screen an index is by trade frequency: keep the cards that change hands most. That is exactly right for Modern — and exactly wrong for Vintage. It is the most important thing to understand about this index.

In Vintage, the names that trade most often and the names that hold the most value are almost opposites.

A Base Set Charizard PSA 10 sells only a handful of times a month — but at five figures, sometimes far more. A common holo sells twenty times a month at $40. Screen on frequency and you would systematically select the cheap commons and throw out the very cards that define vintage. The data is stark: in Vintage, the cards trading ≥10× every 60 days account for only ~20% of dollar volume. The other 80% — the icons — trades rarely but enormously.

So PKMN-VNTG screens on dollar volume, not trade count. Frequency is replaced by a far softer activity floor (just enough that a mark is real), and the index is built around where money actually concentrates — the blue-chips — even though they change hands slowly. This is the single design decision that separates a credible vintage index from a basket of bulk commons.

It also dictates the weekly mark cadence (see §06): a daily mark on a card that trades twice a week would just be a stale price or a single print pretending to be a market.

04Selection — why 229, and not a round number

As with Modern, the component count is not chosen — it falls out of one rule:

Hold the smallest set of liquid, recently-traded names that together cover 90% of the sleeve's trailing-60-day dollar volume.

For Vintage that set is 229 names (132 cover 80% of dollar volume; 333 cover 95%). Counterintuitively that's more components than Modern's 173 — because vintage dollar volume is spread across more blue-chips, whereas modern money piles into a handful of top chase cards.

05Weighting & concentration control

Components are dollar-volume weighted — which also elegantly solves the structural twist: a high-value, low-frequency Charizard earns its weight through price, while a frequently-traded $50 common earns very little. The screen and the weight pull in the same direction.

w_raw(i) = dollar_volume(i) / Σ dollar_volume // trades × median price, 60d
w(i) = cap_waterfall( w_raw , 8% per card , 25% per franchise ) // iterative

Same caps as Modern, applied as an iterative waterfall:

  • 8% per card — Base Charizard cannot become the index.
  • 25% per franchise — every Charizard print across Base, Base 2 and Legendary Collection, capped together.
Result: effective breadth of ~55 names and a top weight of ~9% (an iterative waterfall trims this to a true 8%). Vintage is actually more diversified than Modern at the index level — its dollar volume is less concentrated in a single grail.

06Settlement & the mark

The index settles on completed sales — never asks or model values. Each component's price is a winsorized, cluster-capped median of completed sales, then 14-day smoothed before any return is taken.

// weekly index level — geometric chained returns, weights fixed between rebalances
ret(t) = Σ w(i) · ln( mark(i,t) / mark(i,t-1) )
L(t) = L(t-1) · e^ret(t) // base level 1,000
  • Mark cadence: weekly. The vintage tape is low-frequency by nature; a weekly mark is honest, a daily one would be noise.
  • Rebalance: quarterly, with a divisor for level continuity across membership changes.

07Grade scope — why PSA 8, 9 and 10

This is where Vintage diverges from Modern's grade rules, and for a structural reason: high-grade vintage barely exists. Twenty-plus-year-old cards rarely survive in PSA 10 — vintage PSA 10 is the thinnest tape, not the deepest. The liquid vintage market lives at PSA 8–9. So Vintage includes PSA 8 where Modern does not.

PSA 8 is real, not derived — 6 independent sources, ~$200 median — and it adds breadth precisely in the grades where vintage actually changes hands. By weight it is only ~2% of the index (those are lower-value cards), so it widens the membership without distorting the level. The final grade mix by weight: ~62% PSA 10, ~36% PSA 9, ~2% PSA 8.

Excluded: raw vintage trades at a $7 median — bulk commons, pure noise, out. CGC 10 is a substitute standard (≈ a PSA 9.5 in value) and would fragment the same card across grading bodies.

Vintage has been the momentum leader recently — accelerating hard over the last week and month while Modern cooled. Statistical character:

  • ~27% annualized volatility* — roughly 2.5× Modern's vol. Thin populations and episodic, high-value sales make vintage the higher-beta sleeve.
  • Effective-N ≈ 55 — well diversified despite the thin tape.
  • Correlation with PKMN-MDRN ≈ 0. Vintage and Modern are driven by different forces — nostalgia and fixed scarcity versus print-run and hype cycles — so they are distinct risk factors. Hold them as complementary exposures; a true broad-hobby hedge is a blend of both, not either alone.

09What's deliberately excluded

  • Raw singles out — $7 median, bulk commons; pure noise.
  • Sealed product own index — vintage sealed is real but extremely illiquid; it belongs in a dedicated sealed index, not here.
  • CGC 10 / BGS substitute — same cards under a different standard; breadth without a new risk factor.
  • Japanese vintage phase 2 — a distinct market (different print runs, graded mostly via SNKRDUNK); a candidate for a future JP-vintage sleeve rather than a blend into this one.

10Data & integrity

Every component is an identity-gate-verified card with a cross-venue crosswalk, and every price is a completed sale from the multi-source oracle (alt, TCGplayer, eBay, PokeTrace and others). Because vintage cards often concentrate on a single venue, independence is enforced at the price level rather than the selection level: each mark is winsorized (outlier sales trimmed) and cluster-capped (no single buyer/seller cluster can dominate), so a wash trade or a single eccentric sale can't print into the level. Some vintage prints (e.g. unlimited vs shadowless Base Set variants) are still being separated in the identity layer — prices self-correct as clean sales accumulate.

11Honest caveats

* On the statistics. The vintage volatility and correlation figures come from the deep-coverage tape, which currently spans roughly one quarter, and vintage is internally noisier than Modern over short windows (its split-half coherence is lower — a small-sample artifact that should improve with history and proper smoothing). Treat the risk numbers as directional, not yet risk-grade. The composition — universe, dollar-volume screen, grade scope, caps — is robust today.
